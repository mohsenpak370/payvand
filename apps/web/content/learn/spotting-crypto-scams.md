Crypto scams are not sophisticated. They are repetitive: the same handful of opening moves,
aimed at the fact that payments are final and that whoever holds the keys holds the money. Learn
the openings and you avoid nearly all of them.

They are also common enough to have a number attached. In 2025 the FBI's complaint centre recorded
**more than $11 billion** in reported crypto losses across 181,565 complaints, with investment
fraud accounting for the largest share. Reports like that undercount, because most losses are never
reported at all — which is the argument for recognising the pattern early rather than relying on
getting money back afterwards.

## Red flag: someone contacted you first

Real support does not message you first. Not in a Telegram group, not on X, not in your inbox, not
in a comment under an official post. Accounts impersonating support are usually the top reply to
any complaint thread, with the same avatar and a slightly different handle.

The safe move: close the conversation and go to the project's website yourself, then use the
support route listed there.

## Red flag: they need your recovery phrase

There is no legitimate reason for anyone to have it, ever, for any purpose. "To validate", "to
sync", "to migrate", "to unlock staking", "to verify ownership" — all of it is the same request,
and the same theft.

## Red flag: a site asks you to "connect" and then to sign something unexpected

Connecting a wallet only reveals your address. It should not ask you to approve anything. When a
site does ask for a signature, read it — see [Read before you sign](/learn/checking-before-you-sign).
A page that looks exactly like a wallet you use, on an address you do not quite recognise
(`paypa1.com`, `solana-claim.io`, an extra dot), is a phishing page.

Bookmark the sites you use. Reach them from the bookmark, not from a link in a message.

## Red flag: money that arrives unasked

- **Airdrops you did not earn.** Free tokens appearing in your wallet are bait. The token's site
  will invite you to "claim" or "swap" it, which means signing something that drains your real
  balance.
- **Fake payments in your history.** Someone sends you a token with a name like "USDC" and it
  shows up next to your real balance. It is worthless; the app or site that wants to "help you
  convert it" is the actual attack.
- **Address poisoning.** A scammer sends a tiny amount from an address that starts and ends with
  the same characters as one you pay often, so it appears in your history. Later, when you copy
  an address from history, you copy theirs. Always check the middle of the address, or reuse a
  saved contact.

## Red flag: urgency plus a guaranteed return

"Double your deposit in 24 hours." "Only 10 spots left." "Guaranteed 3% daily." Anything with a
clock and a promise of returns is designed to stop you checking. Real investing has no guaranteed
yield, and anyone who did have a reliable edge would not need your money or your DMs.

The same goes for anyone offering to "recover" funds you have already lost, and for romance or
community friendships that turn into an investment pitch after a few weeks.

## Red flag: the app you installed

Fake wallet apps do reach app stores. Install from the link on the project's own website, check
the publisher name, and be suspicious of an app with a familiar name and thirty downloads.

## A short checklist

1. Did they contact me first? Treat it as hostile until proven otherwise.
2. Do they want my recovery phrase, or a signature I do not understand? Stop.
3. Does the site's address match exactly, character for character?
4. Is there urgency, or a guaranteed return? That is the pressure tactic, not the product.
5. If I am unsure: do nothing. A payment not made can be made later; one made cannot be undone.

If something has already happened, act quickly rather than quietly: move whatever is left in the
compromised wallet to a new wallet with a new recovery phrase, and tell the platform you were
using. Nothing can reverse a confirmed payment, but speed limits the damage —
[the lost-phone checklist](/learn/if-your-phone-is-lost) covers the same steps for a stolen device.
